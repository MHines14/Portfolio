MLB Pitcher Projection Model

A daily-updating projection model for 20–30 starting pitchers that turns FanGraphs game logs, team splits, and park factors into innings, strikeout, and runs-allowed estimates — built to show I can hold a real data pipeline together end to end.

The Problem

Public pitcher projections are published as season-long averages, which quietly assume every start looks like every other start. I wanted to answer a narrower question: given who a pitcher is facing, where the game is being played, and how he has thrown over his last several outings, what should today's line actually look like?

The data made that harder than it sounds. FanGraphs game logs, team split tables, and park factors all identify players and teams differently, so nothing joined cleanly on the first pass. Rain-shortened starts and openers produced one- and two-inning rows that dragged rate stats around badly, and a pitcher who changed teams mid-season appeared under two different team keys. Most of the work was deciding what counted as a usable start before any modeling could begin.

What I Built

  • Pulled starting-pitcher game logs from FanGraphs and loaded them into a single table keyed on player_id and game_date.
  • Normalized team abbreviations against a lookup table so mid-season trades resolved to one pitcher record instead of two.
  • Dropped starts under three innings and flagged opener appearances separately, so shortened outings stopped distorting per-start rates.
  • Wrote a query joining game logs to opponent split tables on team_id and handedness to get the batter profile each pitcher actually faced.
  • Calculated a trailing five-start rolling average for K/9, BB/9, and innings per start using a window function over game_date.
  • Applied ballpark run and home-run factors as a multiplier on the runs-allowed estimate.
  • Built the projection output as a daily-refreshing sheet, sorted by projected strikeouts, with each input column visible beside the result.

Key Decisions & Findings

Why a window function instead of a subquery

The trailing five-start average needs a different window for every row, and the correlated subquery version re-scanned the log table once per start — slow, and harder to read six weeks later. A window function partitioned by pitcher and ordered by date computes all of it in one pass, and the intent is legible on the face of the query.

Why I cut starts under three innings

Rain-shortened games and openers are real appearances, but they describe a decision by the manager rather than the pitcher's form. Leaving them in pulled trailing innings-per-start down for anyone who had a single washed-out outing. Cutting them and flagging openers separately kept the rolling average describing what it claims to.

Park factors mattered less than opponent handedness

I expected the ballpark multiplier to be the largest correction. It was not — the handedness profile of the opposing lineup moved projected strikeouts noticeably more than any park adjustment did. That reordered what I spend effort on: cleaner split data is worth more here than a finer park model.
